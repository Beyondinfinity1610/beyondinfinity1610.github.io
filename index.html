01 — Who is doing this

I got here through engines, not through papers.

The honest version of how I ended up spending my nights staring at electrode traces.

I came to signal processing sideways. I got interested in engines first — specifically in the fact that a modern ECU is making hundreds of decisions a second from sensors that are all, quietly, wrong.

A knock sensor drifts as it heats up. An oxygen sensor ages and its response curve moves. A wheel-speed reading is fine until the tyre pressure changes. None of these are broken — they are just measuring a moving world through hardware that is itself moving. And the ECU still has to decide, right now, whether that vibration is detonation or a bad road surface.

That is the same problem as an EEG electrode. Impedance drifts with sweat. Skull thickness varies between people. The sensor gets re-seated at a slightly different angle every morning. And the model still has to decide whether that burst of rhythmic activity is a seizure or the patient chewing. I did not move from cars to neuroscience. I found the same problem wearing different clothes, and the medical version had higher stakes and worse data.

Almost nobody is working on the part where the sensor is lying to you. That is the part I want.

What I actually enjoy is the diagnostic half — the bit where something is not working and the reason is not in the architecture diagram. Most of my best results this year came from reading my own dataloader properly, inspecting weights I assumed were training, and finding out that a validation set had been shuffled. That is unglamorous and it is where the real movement is.

I am a final-year computer science student at VIT Chennai, and I do most of this outside coursework because it is the part I would be doing anyway.

02 — The problem

Three ways a biosignal model quietly lies to you.

None of these show up in a validation score computed the usual way. I have personally been caught by all three. Everything below is built to attack one of them.

01 / Non-stationarity

The signal changes while you are modelling it.

EEG is not a stationary process. Its spectral content, amplitude and phase structure drift over minutes, and the interesting events are exactly the moments where that drift breaks. A model trained on windowed averages learns the drift and misses the break. The fix is decomposition before learning — mode decomposition, Hilbert envelopes, per-branch filtering — so the network receives separated oscillations instead of their sum.

02 / Domain shift

Subject A does not generalise to Subject B.

Electrode impedance, skull thickness, cortical folding and cognitive baseline all differ. A network with enough capacity will identify the patient and infer the label from that — scoring near-perfectly in cross-validation and collapsing on anyone new. It is the same failure as an engine map tuned on one dyno in one climate. The answer is not more data. It is an architecture that is punished for encoding identity at all.

03 / Artifact vs. event

A jaw clench and a seizure look the same to one sensor.

On behind-the-ear wearable EEG, chewing, speaking and walking produce high-amplitude rhythmic activity that is spectrally indistinguishable from an ictal discharge. Knock detection has exactly this problem: detonation and a pothole hit the same sensor the same way, and the only way through is a second channel that disagrees. Here that channel turned out to be the heart.

NeuroSync · wearable focal-seizure detection

Step 01 — The setting

A sensor behind the ear, and a person living their life.

SeizeIT2: 125 patients wearing a behind-the-ear Sensor Dot in hospital. Two EEG channels instead of a 21-electrode cap. The patient walks, eats, talks and sleeps. Seizures occupy well under one percent of the recording.

The published baselines on this dataset are a classical SVM and ChronoNet. Beating them honestly, with nobody reviewing the output, is the whole task.

125 patients 2-channel bte-EEG 2,912 hours <1% prevalence
Step 02 — What I built first

Five tokenizers, a trust gate, and a cross-modal transformer.

Every modality got the encoder its physics deserved. A CNN-GRU on low-frequency EEG for seizure evolution. Teager energy on the high band. Deltoid EMG reduced to energy and rhythmicity. A masked autoencoder over a Takens embedding of the ECG. ROCKET on the accelerometer, frozen by design.

Above them a gate that emits one scalar of trust in the EEG, and a transformer doing directional cross-attention so the modalities could find their own alignment. It was a good design. I ran thirty-odd experiments on it.

5 tokenizers DynMM gate Deep MulT 4-phase curriculum
Step 03 — The plateau

It stopped moving, and I kept tuning it anyway.

Around 0.57 AUROC on a balanced validation set. Every architectural idea bought a fraction of a point. I had a headline event-F1 of 53% that I was genuinely proud of and quoted in slides.

The mistake was that I kept optimising instead of asking what the model was actually doing. Twenty-eight experiments is a lot of evidence that your problem is not the one you think it is.

~0.57 AUROC 28 tuning runs balanced val
Step 04 — The diagnosis

Three things were wrong, and none of them were the architecture.

The model was reading the last 2 seconds of every 17-second window. One line. Fixing it moved continuous AUROC from 0.51 to 0.72 — the largest single gain of the project.

The fusion residual weights were exactly zero and the gate had collapsed to a constant. What I had been calling multimodal for months was functionally EEG-only. And the continuous validation set was loaded in hash order, so every event-level number I had reported was an artifact of shuffled time.

0.51 → 0.72 AUROC fusion weights = 0 shuffled eval
Step 05 — The rebuild

The heart was carrying the signal the whole time.

Recomputing raw physiology at the detector's firing windows: real seizures average 93 bpm, false alarms 78. Ictal tachycardia, AUROC 0.73. The modalities were never useless — the pipeline had been discarding them.

So: keep the working EEG detector, feed heart rate and motion in explicitly, delete the dead fusion, train it end to end. Event-F1 went from 0.163 to 0.327 with false alarms cut roughly five-fold, and it held under leave-one-patient-out at 0.326.

HR AUROC 0.73 F1 0.163 → 0.327 LOPO 0.326
Step 06 — The fair test

Retrained on the official split, against the published baselines.

Train on subjects 001–096, validate on 097–125, score on the full continuous stream. Continuous AUROC 0.759, best seed 0.780 — past the published SVM at 0.700 and level with ChronoNet at 0.792.

At the operating point: about 60% sensitivity at 13 false alarms per hour, against ChronoNet's 84% at 100.5. Better practical profile, lower sensitivity. And human experts reach roughly 59% on this modality — so the remaining gap is the hardware, not the model.

05 — Second research line

The brain is a graph. So build one per band.

Resting-state EEG classification for ADHD across 121 subjects. Different dataset, same obsession: making sure the network learned the condition rather than the person.

Why five graphs and not one.

Connectivity is computed with the weighted Phase Lag Index rather than correlation. wPLI is phase-based and therefore immune to volume conduction — the artifact where one source appears at several electrodes at once and manufactures connectivity that does not exist. It is the single largest reason EEG connectivity findings fail to replicate.

Delta and gamma carry opposite discriminative patterns in ADHD. Fuse their adjacency matrices early and the two cancel. So each band gets its own Graph Attention Network, message passing independently over its own topology, and the five embeddings are combined only at the end through learned attention. Switch bands below — the change in structure is real, not decorative.

Gradient reversal — the anti-memorisation layer

A second head is attached to the shared encoder and asked to identify which subject the window came from. Between them sits a layer that passes activations forward unchanged and multiplies the gradient by a negative constant on the way back. The subject classifier gets better at its job; the encoder is actively pushed to make that job impossible. What survives is the part of the representation that is not a fingerprint.

07 — How I work

Five rules, most of them learned the bad way.

Every one of these makes the work slower and the headline number smaller. That is the trade, and it is the right one — the alternative is a result that only exists inside its own notebook.

01

Read the dataloader before you touch the architecture.

The biggest gain in my flagship project — 0.51 to 0.72 AUROC — came from noticing the model was reading two seconds of a seventeen-second window. Every clever thing I built before that was tuning a network that could barely see. I now audit the data path first, every time, and it is boring and it keeps working.

02

Inspect the weights you assume are training.

My fusion residual weights were exactly zero for months. The gate had collapsed to a constant. One of its inputs was dead. Nothing in the loss curve indicated any of this. If a component is supposed to be learning, open the checkpoint and confirm that it did.

03

Choose the metric a practitioner would choose.

At sub-one-percent prevalence, accuracy is noise. I score on the full continuous stream rather than a balanced subset, report AUROC because it is roughly fourteen times more stable across bootstrap resamples than sensitivity, and quote sensitivity only alongside its false-alarm rate. And I check whether a suspiciously good number is really coverage in disguise — because once, it was.

04

Split by subject, always.

Random window-level splits leak the patient across the boundary and inflate every score in the paper. Splits are patient-level and seeded; leave-one-patient-out where the dataset allows it; and where a published split exists, use theirs even when yours is kinder.

05

Publish the graveyard, including your own corrections.

A third of my experiments made things worse and they are documented as carefully as the wins. So is the fact that my headline result for most of a year was an artifact. Negative results are the only part of a campaign that cannot be produced by luck, and a correction you publish yourself costs far less than one a reviewer finds.
