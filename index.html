// 01 — About
The Approach

I'm a student researcher at VIT Chennai working at the intersection of Deep Learning and Signal Intelligence. Most of my time is spent on one problem: making AI that actually generalizes.

Standard models hit high accuracy in controlled environments then quietly fall apart in the wild. I'm interested in why that happens and how to build architectures that are structurally resilient to it — what I call the "Generalization Gap."

I work primarily with EEG and Time-Series data — trying to understand the underlying physics of a signal, not just its surface pattern. How phase and amplitude encode information. How connectivity between nodes reveals structure that raw features miss.

I believe in building in public. The dead ends are part of the work.

Current Obsession
Why does cross-subject generalization fail even when in-subject accuracy is high? The model isn't learning the signal. It's learning the person.
Working Hypothesis
Graph-based architectures have an inherent structural advantage for EEG because they can encode spatial dependencies between electrodes — something CNNs approximate but can't represent directly.
Learning Direction
Going deeper into interpretability. Not XAI as a checkbox — but architectures where the decision pathway is mathematically traceable from input to output.
Honest Status
Early in the journey. Documenting everything. The repos show where I've been. Current work stays private until it's ready.

// 03 — Thinking
Notes from the Build
From the ADHD project // Active research note
Subject Mimicry: when the model is right for the wrong reason
Subject v129 — healthy control — gets classified as ADHD with 100% window confidence. The model isn't broken. It's doing exactly what it was trained to do: find high-sync connectivity patterns. The problem is v129's baseline brain connectivity is statistically indistinguishable from the ADHD signature. This isn't a data problem. It's a representation problem. PLV is measuring synchrony — but it can't distinguish real hyper-connectivity from volume conduction artifacts. Switching to PLI (Phase Lag Index) filters out zero-lag correlations caused by field spread. The real lesson: accuracy metrics hide per-subject failure. Always audit at the individual level.
On architecture choice
Why GNNs have a structural prior CNNs don't
EEG electrodes aren't a grid. They're a spatial graph with topology that matters. CNNs treat them as sequences or images — which loses the inter-electrode structure. A Graph Attention Network can encode adjacency by design and learn which electrode relationships actually matter for a given class. The benchmark confirms it: RF at 62%, 1D-CNN at 77.77%, GAT at 77.91%. The gap will widen as the connectivity representation improves.
On quantum-inspired methods
The classifier is just a ruler, not the point
In the Quantum-Hilbert project, the SVM at the end isn't the contribution. It's a probe measuring whether the quantum circuit learned a better representation of the signal. This reframing matters: most people optimize for the downstream metric. The interesting question is whether the feature space itself has better structure. Hilbert transform naturally produces complex-valued outputs — instantaneous phase and amplitude — which align with quantum state amplitudes. That's not a coincidence worth ignoring.
On the learning path
Why I started with SVM before touching deep learning
The Motor Imagery project was deliberate. I could have gone straight to a Transformer. Instead I spent time understanding Mu rhythms, Beta rhythms, Event-Related Desynchronization, and why specific electrodes over the motor cortex carry more information than others. That grounding means that when I look at a GNN attention map now, I know what the model should be attending to — and when it's attending to noise. You can't audit a black box if you don't know what truth looks like.
