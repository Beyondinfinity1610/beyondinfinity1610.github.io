The 30-second version, in words

I built a wearable seizure detector that fused brain, heart, muscle and movement signals, then audited it. It was reading 2 s of each 17 s window, and its fusion weights were exactly zero: the extra signals contributed nothing. I rebuilt it one controlled step at a time and kept two failures on the record. On patients it had never seen it reaches AUROC 0.826 (95% CI 0.782–0.860), above both baselines released with the dataset, and finds 67.4% of seizures at 10 false alarms/hour. That is a lot of false alarms. This is a candidate-event aid, not an alarm.

02ASSUMPTION· did not hold

Fuse everything. The extra signals will help.

The first design was built top-down from reasoning about physiology, not from the data. It is listed here as an assumption because it did not hold.

03SIGNAL

The detail

A behind-the-ear wearable records four things at once: brain activity (EEG), heart activity (ECG), muscle activity (EMG) and movement (accelerometer). Whether the last three earn their place is an empirical question, not an assumption.

Seizures are rare. In a continuous recording almost every window is ordinary, so scoring on balanced snippets grades a different problem from the one a wearable faces.

06CHANGE

Stop adding machinery.

Rebuilt one controlled step at a time: +0.316 AUROC across the ladder. Progress came largely from removing what did not contribute and supplying evidence that did.

Two steps failed. Both stay in the record.

  • An early-fusion design that collapsed.
  • A jaw-muscle feature that made things worse.

08WHAT IT MEANS

A model can look multimodal and not be. Inspect the weights, not only the score, and print the alarm rate next to the sensitivity.

What this shows

  • A fusion model can score as multimodal while its fusion pathway contributes exactly nothing.
  • Inspecting the weights and the window coverage found what the score hid.
  • Re-scoring on the realistic stream changed which design choices looked good.

What this does not show

  • Performance on any other cohort. This is one dataset.
  • A standalone alarm. 10 false alarms/hour is a high alarm burden. It is a candidate-event aid for review.
  • Prediction. The system detects seizures; it does not forecast them.
  • Clinical validation. It is not a deployed system.
  • A head-to-head win over other published work, which uses other cohorts and protocols.
  • An explanation of seizure physiology. It detects; it does not explain.
